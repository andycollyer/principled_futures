# Remediation and redress

**What it measures.** Whether, when AI is found to have treated people unfairly, the organisation corrects the system, re-examines the decisions it influenced and puts the affected people right.

**Why it matters.** Fixing a model helps the next applicant, not the one already refused a job, a loan or a service. Left untold, that person keeps the harm and cannot use the remedies the law provides.

**The UK position.** The 2023 white paper made contestability and redress one of five principles, but said: "The UK’s initial non-statutory approach will not create new rights or new routes to redress at this stage." Redress therefore runs through existing law. After a significant, solely automated decision, the person must be told and be able to make representations, obtain human intervention and contest it. Damage from a data protection infringement brings a right to compensation. Under the Equality Act a court or tribunal can award compensation; an employment tribunal can also recommend steps to reduce the adverse effect on the complainant.

**What good looks like.** A written process covering all three steps, with time to remedy measured, repeat faults tracked, independent assessment, and a public commitment that is kept.

**Reading the answer.** The usual gap is the middle step: nobody works out who was affected. For most organisations, no provision read requires seeking out affected people unprompted; that is the board's choice. FCA-regulated firms must identify and remedy systemic problems in handling complaints (DISP 1.3.3R), should consider contacting customers who have not complained (DISP 1.3.6G) and must act to rectify harm caused (Consumer Duty guidance FG22/5, paragraph 5.15).

**A defensible figure.** Since 19 June 2026 a controller must acknowledge a data protection complaint within 30 days of receipt (Data Protection Act 2018, section 164A(3)).
