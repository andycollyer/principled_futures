# DPIA discipline

**What it measures.** Whether data protection impact assessments are triggered when they should be, done in depth, and acted on: findings change the design, and the assessment is revisited when the system changes.

**Why it matters.** It is where someone must write down what an AI system could do to the people whose data it uses: the applicant wrongly screened out, the teenager confiding in a chatbot, the worker tracked. Done late or thinly, those people carry risks nobody weighed.

**The UK position.** UK GDPR Article 35 requires an assessment before any processing likely to result in a high risk to people. The Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 left that duty unchanged. The Information Commission (ICO) says the great majority of AI uses will meet the trigger; a decision not to assess must be documented. In 2024 the regulator said one firm's early assessments of an AI chatbot contained "a cursory and higher-level risk assessment, in tabular format", with no reasons given. The controller must also review when the risk changes. Failure can bring a fine.

**What good looks like.** Every AI use screened; assessment begun early in design; risks described for the people affected, not the organisation; each mitigation owned and checked as working; senior sign-off; review on material change.

**Reading the answer.** Ask for the assessment's date against the go-live date, and for one design change it caused. The regulator's audit of AI recruitment tools found some assessments completed retrospectively, and most not regularly reviewed after launch.

**A defensible figure.** In the chatbot case the regulator saw five versions. It described the first four as cursory on risk and accepted the fifth, dated 22 November 2023, almost nine months after launch on 27 February 2023.
