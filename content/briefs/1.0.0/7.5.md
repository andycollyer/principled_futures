# Worker information and consultation

**What it measures.** Whether the people who will work with an AI system, or be managed by it, are told before it starts, and whether what they say can still change it.

**Why it matters.** A system that allocates shifts, scores performance or watches activity alters working lives. If it arrives unannounced, workers cannot ask how it judges them, warn where it will go wrong, or object. The harm is to dignity and fair treatment, and it falls on those with least say.

**The UK position.** No UK statute requires consultation on AI as such. Employers must consult employees or their safety representatives in good time on "the health and safety consequences for those employees of the introduction (including the planning thereof) of new technologies into the workplace". Where the statutory information and consultation provisions apply, representatives must be consulted on decisions likely to bring substantial changes in work organisation. Data protection law tells a controller assessing high-risk processing to seek, where appropriate, the views of the people affected, and the Information Commission (ICO) expects employers planning monitoring to seek and record workers' views. The EU AI Act goes further where it applies: employers must inform workers and their representatives before a high-risk system is used at work.

**What good looks like.** Representatives and affected staff briefed before the decision is final; their comments recorded, answered and visibly acted on.

**Reading the answer.** An announcement at launch is the usual weak answer. Ask what changed because of what staff said.

**A defensible figure.** The general right to be informed and consulted covers only undertakings with at least 50 employees and, unless the employer acts first, only once 2% of them, and no fewer than 15, have asked.
