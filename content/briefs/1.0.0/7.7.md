# Acceptable use and shadow AI

**What it measures.** Whether staff know which AI tools they may use and for what, and whether the organisation finds out about the tools it never approved.

**Why it matters.** People paste customer records, staff files and confidential plans into free tools nobody has assessed. The individuals whose information it is never agreed to that and will never know. The organisation cannot protect what it cannot see.

**The UK position.** No law names shadow AI. Data protection law requires a controller to take steps so that anyone acting under its authority handles personal data only on its instructions, and to pass personal data to an outside service only under a binding contract. The National Cyber Security Centre treats unapproved AI as a form of shadow IT. It says such use is rarely malicious and usually shows that approved tools fall short, and it warns: "If you blame or punish staff, their peers will be reluctant to tell you about their own unsanctioned practices". It advises a simple, quick process for requests and tells users not to put sensitive information into public AI tools. Government research found that very few businesses had any internal policy on AI.

**What good looks like.** A short policy written for staff: approved tools, information that must never be entered, a fast route to request something new. Unapproved use found by asking without blame and by technical monitoring, and tracked over time.

**Reading the answer.** A policy nobody has read, backed by a slow approval queue, is the usual weak answer. A ban alone hides the problem.

**A defensible figure.** In a 2025 survey across 47 countries, 70% of employees who use AI at work said they use free, publicly available tools.
