# Workforce impact assessment

**What it measures.** Whether the effect on jobs is assessed in writing before the board decides to adopt AI that changes work, and whether the board's consideration is minuted.

**Why it matters.** People whose tasks, pay or posts will change tend to learn last. An assessment made beforehand lets redeployment, retraining or redesign be weighed while they are still options. Made afterwards, it is an explanation, not a safeguard.

**The UK position.** Directors must act to promote the success of the company and, in doing so, have regard among other matters to "the interests of the company's employees". Companies must record minutes of directors' meetings. Larger companies must publish a statement of how directors had regard to those matters, and the UK Corporate Governance Code asks boards to describe how they were considered in board discussions and decision-making. No UK law requires a workforce impact assessment for AI by that name, and the duty is owed to the company, not to staff. Redundancies proposed on a sufficient scale trigger collective consultation. Acas advises consulting workers and their representatives on introducing AI, and notes that expecting roles to use it could change terms and conditions.

**What good looks like.** A dated assessment before approval: roles touched, tasks removed and added, what happens to the people, transition costs, alternatives considered. The board's discussion is minuted. Predicted effects are later compared with actual ones.

**Reading the answer.** Papers dated after the decision are the weak answer. So is an assessment that never mentions job losses.

**A defensible figure.** In the CIPD's autumn 2025 survey of 2,019 UK employers, 17% expected the use of AI tools to reduce their headcount in the next 12 months.
