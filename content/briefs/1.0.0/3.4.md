# Special-category data controls

**What it measures.** Whether the organisation stops sensitive personal data from driving automated decisions about people unless a lawful exception applies, and whether that rule is enforced.

**Why it matters.** Health, ethnicity, religion, sexual orientation, trade union membership and biometric identifiers are the facts most easily turned into discrimination. When a system decides a job, a loan or a service on them with nobody looking, the person may never learn why, and the harm falls on those already most exposed.

**The UK position.** Since 5 February 2026 the UK GDPR has allowed most automated decisions, subject to safeguards. Sensitive data is the exception: "A significant decision based entirely or partly on processing described in Article 9(1) (processing of special categories of personal data) may not be taken based solely on automated processing, unless one of the following conditions is met." The conditions are explicit consent, or a decision necessary for a contract or required or authorised by law, each of the last two also needing a substantial public interest condition. The regulator's draft guidance, not yet final, applies the rule even to a small amount of such data, including inferred data.

**What good looks like.** A documented rule; every automated decision checked for sensitive data, inferred data included; technical blocks where possible; and an exceptions register, each entry recording its condition, reviewed by the board.

**Reading the answer.** The usual gap is inference. A system with no field marked health can still treat people differently on a health inference, which the regulator counts as special category data however uncertain the inference.

**A defensible figure.** The regulator's draft guidance counts 23 substantial public interest conditions; one must apply wherever the contract or legal-authority route is used.
