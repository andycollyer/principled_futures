# Data quality and minimisation

**What it measures.** Whether data fed to AI systems meets a written standard: relevant to the purpose, representative of the people decided about, accurate, and no more than the purpose requires.

**Why it matters.** Every unnecessary field can leak, be misused or tilt a decision; out-of-date records produce wrong outcomes for people. The Information Commission (ICO) is blunt: "You must not collect personal data on the off-chance that it might be useful in the future". The opposite failure harms too: strip out too much and a system may serve some groups worse than others.

**The UK position.** UK GDPR Article 5 requires personal data to be adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary; accurate and, where necessary, kept up to date; and kept in identifiable form no longer than necessary. Article 25 requires that, by default, only data necessary for each purpose is processed. Adequacy cuts both ways: enough data, and no more. ICO guidance, under review since the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025, accepts that minimisation may need balancing against statistical accuracy and non-discrimination. These duties cover personal data only.

**What good looks like.** For each system, a written list of data fields allowed in and kept out, with reasons; a retention schedule with evidence of deletion; checks that data stays current and representative; quality measures tracked with alerts and passed to fairness testing.

**Reading the answer.** Cleaning data only when errors cost money scores low. Ask who decided what each system needs, and when anything was last deleted.

**A defensible figure.** The regulator's 2024 audits of AI recruitment tools found candidate data was usually kept for one or two years after the vacancy closed (page 15); several providers meant to keep their databases indefinitely.
