# Synthetic-content marking

**What it measures.** Whether text, images, audio and video produced by the organisation's AI carry a mark that software can detect, and a visible label where the law requires one.

**Why it matters.** Synthetic content that passes as real deceives the person who sees it and harms the person depicted: fraud, false endorsement, words never spoken. A caption is lost when the content is copied on. The European code of practice asks providers for "at least two layers of machine-readable marking": signed metadata and an imperceptible watermark.

**The UK position.** There is no general UK duty. The government said in March 2026 that the UK does not regulate how AI-generated content is labelled, and proposed to work with industry. The advertising regulator says there is no blanket requirement to disclose AI in adverts, though the rules on misleading content still apply. The EU duty reaches organisations outside the Union whose systems are sold there or whose output is used there. It applies from 2 August 2026; systems already on the market have until 2 December 2026. Those who publish must disclose deepfakes, and AI-written text on matters of public interest that no editor has reviewed.

**What good looks like.** An inventory of systems that generate content; signed metadata and watermarking on their output; checks that the marks survive the organisation's publishing process; a record of what was feasible and what was chosen.

**Reading the answer.** Visible labels alone are the common middle answer; they are not marking. A high answer should rest on test evidence, not a supplier's assurance.

**A defensible figure.** In 2025 Ofcom tested three openly available watermarking tools against 26 types of edit; some edits, such as cropping, removed the watermark.
