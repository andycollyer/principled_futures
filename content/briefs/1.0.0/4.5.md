# Explainability by design

**What it measures.** Whether the ability to explain an AI system's outputs is settled before the system is bought or built: written into the specification and the contract, and kept current.

**Why it matters.** A person refused a job, a loan or a service by a system nobody in the organisation can explain cannot be told why, and so cannot challenge a wrong or unfair result. The harm falls on that person, not the supplier. The regulator's guidance is direct: "you as the data controller have the primary responsibility for ensuring that the AI system you use is capable of producing an appropriate explanation for the decision recipient."

**The UK position.** Data protection law requires safeguards to be built in when the means of processing are chosen, not only afterwards. Where a significant decision is taken solely by automated means, the person must be given information about it and be able to make representations, obtain human intervention and contest it. Both duties sit with the organisation as controller. Regulator guidance, currently under review, says requirements should be specified at the procurement stage; government buyers are told to put explainability in the invitation to tender.

**What good looks like.** Explainability as a selection criterion; supplier documentation obtained before adoption and sufficient for the organisation's own explanations; a contractual right to updates when the model changes; a recorded justification wherever an opaque model is chosen; the same standard for systems built in-house.

**Reading the answer.** The usual weak point is a tool chosen on accuracy and price, the explanation question first asked after a complaint.

**A defensible figure.** Of the widely used algorithmic techniques the regulator's guidance lists, it classes 11 as largely interpretable and four as broadly opaque.
