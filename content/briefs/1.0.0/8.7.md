# Sustainability governance

**What it measures.** Whether one senior person is answerable for sustainability, and whether the board sees performance figures on a set timetable.

**Why it matters.** When nobody owns it, the costs a business puts on its community and the environment, including the energy its AI uses, are nobody's job to weigh. Promises made to customers and lenders have no one answerable for keeping them.

**The UK position.** Every director must have regard to the impact of the company's operations on the community and the environment (Companies Act 2006, section 172); the courts leave the weighing to directors acting in good faith. Companies with more than 500 employees that are traded, banks, insurers, on AIM or have turnover above £500 million must describe their governance of climate-related risks. Smaller companies have no such duty, but a supplier bidding for a major government contract needs its Carbon Reduction Plan approved by the board and signed by a director. Reviewing first-year climate disclosures, the Financial Reporting Council found "the extent of the integration of the climate governance process with the company’s overall governance process was often unclear".

**What good looks like.** A named director or equivalent; sustainability in their objectives; a board paper with metrics at set intervals; authority to refuse a target the business cannot evidence.

**Reading the answer.** Ask who, and when the board last saw the figures. A name without a date is a title, not ownership. In a small firm the owner may also run finance or operations; that is fine.

**A defensible figure.** Of 20 companies whose climate disclosures the Financial Reporting Council reviewed, only half presented all the required information on targets and progress against them (January 2025 review, sections 1 and 3).
