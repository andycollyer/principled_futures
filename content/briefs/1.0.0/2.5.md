# Risk treatment

**What it measures.** Whether every AI risk the organisation has identified carries a recorded decision (reduce it, share it, stop the activity or accept it) with a named person, a date and a check that the action worked.

**Why it matters.** A risk that is written down and then left is a known harm waiting for its victim: the applicant wrongly screened out, the customer wrongly refused credit. A register without decisions shows only that the organisation knew.

**The UK position.** No UK statute prescribes treatment options for AI. The government's Orange Book lists them: avoiding the risk, changing its likelihood or consequences, sharing it through contracts, or "retaining the risk by informed decision". It asks who is accountable for approving each option. The Information Commission (ICO) accepts that not every data protection risk must be eliminated, but a high risk that remains must go to the regulator before processing starts. Sharing has limits. The Equality Act 2010 treats what an agent does with a principal's authority as done by the principal, and no contract term can exclude the Act; whether this reaches a software supplier is untested. Insurance is uncertain: the Lloyd's Market Association says cover may turn on the policy's cyber clause.

**What good looks like.** Each risk has a decision, an owner, a date and a measure of success. Acceptance is signed by someone with authority. Residual risk is reported against appetite.

**Reading the answer.** Pick a risk logged six months ago and ask what changed. Silence is not acceptance.

**A defensible figure.** In a 2025 Lloyd's Market Association survey (144 responses, mostly underwriters), 18% of responses judged insureds' AI risk management inadequate. This is opinion, not claims data.
