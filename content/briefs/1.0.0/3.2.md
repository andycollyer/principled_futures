# Bias in data

**What it measures.** Whether data is examined for inherited unfairness before it informs decisions about people: where it came from, who is missing, and which ordinary-looking fields stand in for protected characteristics.

**Why it matters.** A system trained on past decisions repeats them. Where past lending was prejudiced against women, the Information Commissioner's Office warns, "any model based on such training data is likely to reproduce the same pattern of discrimination." A thinly represented group is served worse. Those harmed are people already disadvantaged, refused again by a process that looks neutral. Deleting the sensitive column is no remedy: occupation, working hours or postcode can carry the same signal.

**The UK position.** Indirect discrimination under the Equality Act 2010 turns on effect; a biased dataset is no defence. Checking for bias often needs sensitive data. The Data Protection Act 2018 permits processing of data on race, religion, health and sexual orientation to keep equality of opportunity under review, provided it is necessary, is not used for decisions about a particular person, and a policy document is in place. Guessing is no substitute: the regulator found gender and ethnicity inferred from names too inaccurate to monitor bias.

**What good looks like.** A documented screening step covering provenance, representation and proxies, completed before the data is used; results logged and repeated as data changes; the method independently assured; the legal condition for bias-monitoring data recorded.

**Reading the answer.** Removing the sensitive fields alone is a low answer, not a high one. Buyers should ask suppliers how training data was checked.

**A defensible figure.** No defensible figure was found.
