# Meaningful human review

**What it measures.** Whether the people who review AI-supported decisions can change them: competent to judge the output, authorised to overrule it, and able to see how it was reached.

**Why it matters.** A reviewer who passes on the system's proposal protects no one. The person refused a job or a loan believes someone considered their case when nobody did. The regulator calls the cause automation bias: people trust the output and stop using their own judgement.

**The UK position.** Since February 2026 the UK GDPR has said "a decision is based solely on automated processing if there is no meaningful human involvement in the taking of the decision". The statute does not define meaningful; it requires only that the extent of profiling be weighed. The practical test is the Information Commissioner's: can the reviewer exercise real influence before the decision is applied, with the authority, discretion and competence to alter it? Access to the system's reasoning is not in the statute; the regulator treats outputs reviewers cannot interpret as a risk that review becomes empty. Where a significant decision has no meaningful involvement, the person must be told and able to make representations, obtain human intervention and contest it.

**What good looks like.** Reviewers trained and authorised in writing to overrule; reasons and inputs visible to them; acceptance and rejection rates monitored; reviews sampled and re-checked; results fed into training and design.

**Reading the answer.** Reviewers who routinely agree and cannot show they assessed the case may be treated as absent. Reviewing only high scorers is a common gap.

**A defensible figure.** The regulator's March 2026 recruitment report drew on evidence from over 30 employers and found many likely relying on solely automated decisions.
