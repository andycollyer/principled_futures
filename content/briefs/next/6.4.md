# Containment and kill-switch

**What it measures.** Whether each AI system can be stopped quickly, by people who have the authority to do it and know how, and whether that has been tried.

**Why it matters.** A system that goes wrong keeps going until someone stops it. Each further hour means more wrong decisions, disclosed data or unwanted actions, against people who cannot stop it themselves. Systems that act, rather than advise, do this faster.

**The UK position.** No UK law requires an off switch for AI by name. Two rules come close. Employers in Great Britain must ensure, where appropriate, that work equipment has readily accessible emergency stop controls (Provision and Use of Work Equipment Regulations 1998, regulation 16); this reaches AI only where it drives such equipment. Firms engaged in algorithmic trading must be able to cancel unexecuted orders immediately in an emergency. The government's voluntary Code of Practice on AI cyber security requires tested incident and recovery plans. Interim advice from the National Cyber Security Centre, published 20 August 2026, says organisations should always be able to halt autonomous AI agent activity immediately. The EU AI Act will require high-risk systems to let a person "interrupt the system through a 'stop' button or a similar procedure that allows the system to come to a halt in a safe state".

**What good looks like.** For each system: the conditions that trigger a stop, the roles allowed to order it, the technical means, and what the business does meanwhile. More than one person can do it. It is rehearsed and timed. Systems that take actions get a containment design before going live.

**Reading the answer.** The weak answer is that one person knows how. Ask when it was last tried and how long it took.

**A defensible figure.** The AI Security Institute, examining 177,436 publicly available tools for AI agents, found that tools which take actions rose from 27% to 65% of usage in 16 months.
