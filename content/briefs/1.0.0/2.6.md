# Incident readiness and suspension

**What it measures.** Whether the organisation would notice an AI system causing harm, has someone with authority to stop it, and knows whom it must tell and how quickly.

**Why it matters.** Every hour a faulty system runs, more people are wrongly refused or put at risk. If only one absent person can switch it off, the harm continues. A late report leaves other users exposed.

**The UK position.** The UK has no AI-specific incident reporting law; existing duties apply by subject. A personal data breach must be reported to the Information Commission (ICO) without undue delay. A producer or distributor who knows a consumer product is unsafe must notify an enforcement authority at once. Under the EU AI Act, where deployers have reason to think a system presents a risk, "they shall, without undue delay, inform the provider or distributor and the relevant market surveillance authority, and shall suspend the use of that system". A serious incident is reported first to the provider. The Commission's consolidated text applies these duties to listed high-risk uses from 2 December 2027.

**What good looks like.** A written procedure names who may suspend each system, and a deputy. Triggers are defined. Reporting duties are mapped in advance. Logs are preserved. Incidents reach the board. The procedure is rehearsed.

**Reading the answer.** Ask who could stop the system tonight, and whether they have ever done so in a test.

**A defensible figure.** The EU AI Act's outer limit for reporting a serious incident is 15 days after the provider or, where applicable, the deployer becomes aware of it; 10 days after a death; two days for a widespread infringement or irreversible disruption of critical infrastructure (Article 73).
