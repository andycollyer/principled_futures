# Vendor fairness assurance

**What it measures.** Whether a supplier's statement that its AI is fair is tested before the organisation relies on it: evidence asked for, read, re-checked, and backed by contract terms that give a right to look.

**Why it matters.** Applicants, customers and staff are scored or filtered by a tool the organisation did not build and cannot see inside. If it treats a group worse, they bear the harm, and they have no dealings with the supplier.

**The UK position.** The Equality Act 2010 places the duty not to discriminate on the employer and the service provider. Whether a software supplier counts as their agent is undecided, so buying a tool should not be assumed to move the duty. Under the public sector equality duty the Court of Appeal has ruled: a police force that relied on the manufacturer, without independent verification that the software was free of bias, breached that duty (Bridges v South Wales Police [2020] EWCA Civ 1058, paragraphs 199 to 201). The ruling covers public bodies and public functions only. Data protection law requires a controller to use only processors giving sufficient guarantees, under a contract allowing audits and inspections; that right covers data protection compliance, so a wider fairness warranty has to be negotiated.

**What good looks like.** The Information Commission (ICO) tells recruiters: "Request test results or reports and evidence of actions AI providers have taken to address fairness, accuracy, or bias issues in AI tools or outputs." Government guidance adds a pilot on the organisation's own data before deployment.

**Reading the answer.** The usual weak point is evidence requested and filed unread. A high answer needs someone able to judge the test results, and a record of what was done when the supplier last changed the model.

**A defensible figure.** Auditing providers of AI recruitment tools, the regulator made 296 recommendations; 97% were accepted in full and 3% in part.
