# Board-level security assurance

**What it measures.** Whether the board holds evidence that its controls over AI work, enough to support a public statement that the company's material controls are effective.

**Why it matters.** A declaration tells investors, staff and customers that the company's safeguards work. If the board signs it without evidence on AI, those people rely on an assurance nobody tested, and failures that harm them (unfair decisions, leaked data, wrong numbers) surface later and larger.

**The UK position.** The UK Corporate Governance Code 2024 asks boards of listed companies to declare in the annual report whether material controls were effective at the balance sheet date, and to describe any that were not. The provision applies from 2026 financial years, on a comply-or-explain basis. The Financial Reporting Council does not define a material control; the board decides. Its guidance says these could include controls over "information and technology risks including cybersecurity, data protection and new technologies (e.g. artificial intelligence)". It adds that a board can give only a reasonable conclusion based on evidence obtained, and that external assurance is not required. Unlisted companies are outside the Code; the government's Cyber Governance Code asks any board for formal reporting at least quarterly.

**What good looks like.** AI systems considered when material controls are identified; evidence that each control operates, gathered through the year; weaknesses reported with the action taken.

**Reading the answer.** The weak answer is that management has confirmed it. Ask what evidence on AI controls the board saw, and who tested it.

**A defensible figure.** In the Financial Reporting Council's 2025 review of 100 annual reports, 40 companies did not report the outcome of their review of control effectiveness.
