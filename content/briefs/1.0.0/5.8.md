# Records and accountability

**What it measures.** Whether the organisation can prove, from records made at the time, how each AI system uses personal data: scope, legal basis, risk assessment, safeguards and decisions taken.

**Why it matters.** A person refused a loan or a job by an AI system can only have that outcome checked if someone can show what was done and why. Without records, mistakes cannot be traced or put right, and nobody outside can confirm the person was treated fairly. The regulator warns: "if you can’t show good data protection practices, it may leave you open to fines and reputational damage."

**The UK position.** Data protection law makes the organisation responsible for compliance and for being able to demonstrate it. The Information Commission (ICO) can require information and, since August 2025, documents, and can inspect premises and equipment. Since February 2026 it can also require a report, by a person it approves, at the organisation's expense and, where it suspects a breach, call current or former staff and managers to answer questions. Ignoring a notice can itself be fined. Ordinary cooperation earns no reduction in a fine: the regulator treats it as required by law.

**What good looks like.** One file per system, kept current, in a known place, which someone other than its author can produce. The regulator's auditors ask first for policies, risk registers and records of processing, then test whether practice matches.

**Reading the answer.** The usual weak point is evidence that exists but is scattered, or assembled only when a request arrives.

**A defensible figure.** An urgent information notice can require documents 24 hours after it is given; an urgent assessment notice, seven days. In 2025/26 the regulator completed 85 audits, risk reviews and follow-ups.
