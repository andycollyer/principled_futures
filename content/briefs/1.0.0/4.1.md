# AI-interaction disclosure

**What it measures.** Whether people are told, at the point of contact, that they are dealing with an AI system and not a person, under a written standard covering every channel.

**Why it matters.** People who think a person is listening disclose more and trust more. Letting that belief stand is deception by silence, and it falls hardest on children, older people and anyone in distress. The Competition and Markets Authority puts the test simply: "if the fact they are dealing with AI rather than a person might affect people’s decisions then you should tell them."

**The UK position.** No UK statute names a duty to disclose AI. The duty arises from consumer law: leaving out information the average consumer needs to take an informed decision is a misleading omission, and the business answers for its AI agent even where a supplier built it. Fines can reach £300,000 or, if higher, 10% of turnover. Transparency is one of the government's five non-statutory AI principles. In the European Union, an explicit duty has applied since 2 August 2026. It falls on the system's provider, and the exception for obvious cases is read restrictively.

**What good looks like.** Disclosure at the first exchange, in the interface or spoken aloud, in plain words. A line in the terms and conditions is not enough. Coverage is checked across web chat, voice, messaging and email, and the wording is tested with users.

**Reading the answer.** The usual gap is the channel nobody listed. A fluent assistant handling complaints is where disclosure matters most.

**A defensible figure.** In a March 2024 survey for the UK's AI Safety Institute, 68% agreed that a customer service chatbot should make clear whether the agent is human or artificial; 16% disagreed.
