# Incident response integration

**What it measures.** Whether a failing AI system triggers the organisation's existing incident response: named scenarios, known reporting duties, rehearsal, and lessons fed back.

**Why it matters.** A model can give wrong or unfair answers for weeks with no alarm. If nobody treats that as an incident, the people refused, overcharged or exposed are not told and not put right.

**The UK position.** No UK law sets a general duty to report AI failures. Where personal data is involved, a breach must be notified to the Information Commission (ICO) within 72 hours where feasible, and to individuals when the risk to them is high. The government's voluntary AI security code says "Developers and System Operators shall create, test and maintain an AI system incident management plan and an AI system recovery plan." Under the EU AI Act, providers of high-risk systems must report a serious incident within 15 days of awareness (10 after a death; two for a widespread infringement or irreversible disruption of critical infrastructure); deployers tell the provider first. The Commission's consolidated copy of 27 July 2026 shows that article unamended, and gives 2 August 2026 as the Act's general application date, with later dates for high-risk requirements.

**What good looks like.** AI failure types written into the plan; reporting duties mapped beforehand; logs preserved; an AI scenario exercised yearly; each incident reviewed and lessons applied.

**Reading the answer.** The usual weak answer is that it would be handled like any outage. Ask when an AI scenario was last rehearsed, and what changed afterwards.

**A defensible figure.** The 2025/2026 government breaches survey found that 25% of UK businesses had a formal incident response plan, and 45% had none of the response measures listed. It covers cyber incidents generally, not AI.
