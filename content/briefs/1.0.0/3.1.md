# Discrimination risk awareness

**What it measures.** Whether the organisation knows where its AI could treat people worse because of who they are: each AI use set against the protected characteristics it could affect, with a named owner.

**Why it matters.** A model can discriminate without anyone intending it, and without holding any protected data. The Information Commissioner's Office puts it plainly: "These 'proxy variables' enable the model to reproduce patterns of discrimination associated with those characteristics, even if the designers did not intend this." Removing the sensitive fields does not remove the risk.

**The UK position.** The Equality Act 2010 protects nine characteristics. Indirect discrimination turns on effect, not motive: a practice that puts a protected group at a particular disadvantage is unlawful unless it is a proportionate means of achieving a legitimate aim. The duty sits with the employer or service provider applying the practice, and anything done by an agent with the organisation's authority is treated as its own act, whether or not it knew. Data protection law adds a separate fairness duty: no unjustified adverse effects on individuals. The regulator has made automated decisions in recruitment a stated priority.

**What good looks like.** A documented map, refreshed when a use changes, reported to the board, independently reviewed, and consulted before purchase or deployment. Government guidance on recruitment expects an impact assessment before procurement and again before deployment.

**Reading the answer.** Awareness confined to HR is the usual weak point; pricing, lending and service triage carry the same exposure. A low answer here limits what the organisation can credibly claim about bias testing and monitoring.

**A defensible figure.** Auditing AI recruitment tools in 2024, the regulator made 296 recommendations to developers and providers; 97% were accepted and 3% partially accepted.
