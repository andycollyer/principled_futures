# Roles and decision rights

**What it measures.** Whether each use of AI has a named person answerable for what it does, with the authority to change or stop it, and a known route for taking concerns upward.

**Why it matters.** The supplier assumes the customer governs use, IT assumes the business owns the outcome, and the business assumes someone senior approved it. A person wrongly refused, flagged or dismissed then finds nobody who can put it right.

**The UK position.** The government's cross-sector principle, which is not statute and is applied by existing regulators, reads: "Governance measures should be in place to ensure effective oversight of the supply and use of AI systems, with clear lines of accountability established across the AI life cycle." No general law requires a named owner for AI. Financial services comes closest. The Financial Conduct Authority says that in larger firms any use of AI falls within an existing senior manager's responsibilities; most respondents to the regulators' 2022 discussion paper opposed a separate responsibility for AI. The Prudential Regulation Authority expects banks with internal model approval to name an accountable senior manager for model risk. Government guidance tells departments to nominate a senior responsible owner for each AI project.

**What good looks like.** For each system, a written allocation: who is accountable, who operates it, who is consulted before change, where escalation goes. Escalations are logged and reviewed.

**Reading the answer.** Ask who could switch a given system off today. A committee is not an owner.

**A defensible figure.** The National Audit Office found that 24 of the 32 government bodies with deployed AI said their use cases always or usually had a named accountable owner (March 2024, paragraph 1.10).
