# Access and change control

**What it measures.** Whether the organisation knows who can change what an AI system does (its model, prompts, training data and data pipelines), limits that to those who need it, and keeps a record of every change.

**Why it matters.** A change to a prompt or a data source can alter decisions about many people at once, with no change to any code. If nobody can say who made the change, or when, the people harmed cannot get an explanation, and the organisation cannot put it right or show it was not deliberate.

**The UK position.** No UK law is specific to AI here. The government's voluntary Code of Practice on AI cyber security says developers and operators shall review their access controls and secure models, data and pipelines, and that developers should keep "an audit log of changes to system prompts or other model configuration (including prompts) that affect the underlying working of the systems". Operators shall log system and user actions. Where personal data is involved, UK GDPR requires appropriate security, and that anyone with access to the data acts only on the controller's instructions.

**What good looks like.** Named individuals, not shared log-ins, each with no more access than the role needs. Changes to prompts, models and data sources are versioned, attributable and, for consequential systems, approved before release. Suppliers give notice of their changes. Access is reviewed on a schedule.

**Reading the answer.** Ask who last changed the system prompt, and when. A slow or uncertain answer is the finding.

**A defensible figure.** Research published by the AI Security Institute found that 250 poisoned documents were enough to plant a hidden trigger in a language model, at every model and dataset size tested.
