# Decision-specific explanation

**What it measures.** Whether the organisation can tell a person why an AI-supported decision about them came out as it did: the factors that mattered in their case, in plain words.

**Why it matters.** Someone refused a loan or a job who gets a standard paragraph cannot tell whether the decision was wrong or what to dispute. Their right to challenge exists on paper only. The regulator's draft guidance says of the information owed: "This is decision-specific information about the actual outcome, rather than simply repeating information you provide in your privacy notice".

**The UK position.** Where a significant decision is solely automated, the UK GDPR requires safeguards that include giving the person information about that decision, fully in force since February 2026. Privacy notices and replies to access requests must separately give meaningful information about the logic involved. The statute does not say how much detail is owed; the regulator's reading is still a consultation draft. Its earlier guidance with the Alan Turing Institute, now under review, names six types of explanation and separates explaining the process from explaining the outcome.

**What good looks like.** For each kind of significant decision, an agreed explanation format completed case by case: contributing factors, data used, who to ask for a review. Explanations sampled for accuracy and tested with the people who receive them.

**Reading the answer.** Privacy-notice wording alone is the usual weak answer. An organisation that cannot see why a bought-in system decided as it did cannot explain it, yet stays responsible.

**A defensible figure.** In the 2024 Bank of England and Financial Conduct Authority survey of 118 financial firms, 81% of those using AI employed some explainability method (section 2.7): a method, not an explanation to the person affected.
