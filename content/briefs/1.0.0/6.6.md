# Resilience and recovery

**What it measures.** Whether the business can keep serving people when an AI system is unavailable, withdrawn or switched off, and how quickly it can recover.

**Why it matters.** When a system that screens claims, answers customers or schedules care stops, the people waiting bear the cost: payments delayed, questions unanswered, decisions stalled. A fallback that exists only on paper protects no one.

**The UK position.** No general law requires an AI service to be kept running. Where personal data is processed, security must include the ability to restore access to it in a timely manner after an incident. Regulated financial firms must identify their important business services, set impact tolerances and stay within them through severe but plausible disruption; they had until 31 March 2025. Since 13 July 2026 four large cloud providers have been overseen as critical third parties, but firms keep responsibility for their own third-party risk. The National Cyber Security Centre's framework for essential services asks that "consideration is also given to how operation of the essential function can continue in the event of technology failure or compromise".

**What good looks like.** For each AI system the business depends on: a written manual fallback, a decision on what stops first, a recovery target, and a test that proves them. Reliance on a single supplier is named and owned.

**Reading the answer.** A supplier's uptime guarantee answers a different question. Ask when the fallback was last run by the people who would have to run it.

**A defensible figure.** In the government's 2025/2026 breaches survey, 33% of UK businesses had a business continuity plan that covered cyber security.
