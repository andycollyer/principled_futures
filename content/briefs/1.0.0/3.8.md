# Remediation and redress

**What it measures.** Whether, when AI is found to have treated people unfairly, the organisation corrects the system, re-examines the decisions it influenced and puts the affected people right.

**Why it matters.** Fixing a model helps the next applicant, not the one already refused a job, a loan or a service. Left untold, that person keeps the harm and cannot use the remedies the law provides. A quiet fix leaves the cost of the error with the people least able to detect it.

**The UK position.** The 2023 white paper made contestability and redress one of five principles, but said: "The UK’s initial non-statutory approach will not create new rights or new routes to redress at this stage." Redress therefore runs through existing law. For a significant decision taken solely by automated processing, the person must be given information and be able to make representations, obtain human intervention and contest it. Anyone who suffers damage from a data protection infringement has a right to compensation. Under the Equality Act, a tribunal can award compensation and recommend steps to reduce the adverse effect on the complainant. Since 19 June 2026 controllers must also respond to data protection complaints and report the outcome.

**What good looks like.** A written process covering all three steps, with time to remedy measured, repeat faults tracked, independent assessment, and a public commitment that is kept.

**Reading the answer.** The usual gap is the middle step: nobody works out who was affected. None of the provisions read requires an organisation to seek out affected people unprompted; that is a commitment the board chooses.

**A defensible figure.** A controller must acknowledge a data protection complaint within 30 days of receipt (Data Protection Act 2018, section 164A(3)).
