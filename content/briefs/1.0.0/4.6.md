# Internal transparency

**What it measures.** Whether the organisation can describe its own AI: a complete, current record of each system: purpose, a plain summary of how it works, data used, named owner.

**Why it matters.** Every explanation given to a customer, employee or regulator draws on what the organisation knows about itself. Where that is partial, people are told things nobody has checked, and faults go uncorrected in systems nobody knows are there. The regulator's audit framework for AI states the risk: "Without fully understanding how information is processed, neither provider nor deployer can assure themselves that they have an effective information governance regime in place."

**The UK position.** No UK law requires an AI register by name. Data protection law comes closest: a controller must keep a written record of its processing of personal data, including purposes and categories of data, though not how a system works or who owns it. Central government goes further: since February 2024 departments must publish a record of each algorithmic tool that significantly influences decisions affecting the public. For listed companies, the governance code asks boards to declare whether material controls are effective, for financial years beginning from 1 January 2026.

**What good looks like.** One register covering every system, including tools staff adopted without approval, written so the owner can explain each entry aloud, reviewed on a set cycle and independently checked.

**Reading the answer.** The usual weak points are knowledge held in individuals' heads and unapproved tools no register sees.

**A defensible figure.** The record-keeping duty applies in full at 250 or more staff; smaller organisations are exempt only where processing is occasional, poses no likely risk to people's rights and involves no special category or criminal offence data.
