# Ethical review

**What it measures.** Whether there is a defined point before deployment where ethical concerns are heard, and where the reviewers can require changes or refuse outright.

**Why it matters.** The people harmed by a poor AI deployment (customers scored, staff monitored, applicants filtered out) are rarely in the room when it is approved. A gate is how their interests are weighed before the harm, not after. Without power to refuse, review only records concerns about a decision already made.

**The UK position.** No UK law requires an ethics committee, but the law does supply one hard gate. Where processing of personal data is likely to result in a high risk to people, the controller must assess the impact before processing begins. If a high risk cannot be reduced, the regulator must be consulted first. Its guidance is blunt: "You cannot go ahead with the processing until you have consulted us." The regulator may then limit or ban the processing. Breach of these duties can attract a fine of up to £8,700,000 or 2% of worldwide turnover, whichever is higher. That gate covers data protection only. Wider ethical questions are left to the organisation; government's framework for public bodies accepts that sometimes the right choice is not to use a system at all.

**What good looks like.** Written criteria, a rule that in-scope uses pass through, minutes of what was weighed, board visibility, and outside input on contested calls. UNESCO's 2021 recommendation asks states to introduce ethical impact assessment for AI.

**Reading the answer.** Ask whether the gate has ever refused or changed anything, and who can overrule it.

**A defensible figure.** On prior consultation the regulator has up to eight weeks to give written advice, extendable by six.
