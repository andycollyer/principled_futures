# Third-party and procurement governance

**What it measures.** Whether bought-in AI is examined before adoption and for as long as it is used: questions asked, answers tested, terms written into the contract, and a way out if the supplier fails.

**Why it matters.** When a bought tool gets a decision wrong, the person refused a job, a loan or a service has no dealings with the supplier and cannot see inside the product. If the buyer never checked, nobody did.

**The UK position.** Responsibility stays with the buyer. Government has said it is common for the law to place liability on the last actor in the chain. The Prudential Regulation Authority tells banks and insurers: "Firms that enter into outsourcing arrangements remain fully accountable for complying with all their regulatory obligations." It expects due diligence on every third-party arrangement and, for material outsourcing, audit rights and a tested exit plan. A public body letting a contract above £5 million must generally set and publish at least three key performance indicators. Elsewhere, beyond data protection duties, the expectations are guidance.

**What good looks like.** The Information Commission (ICO) audit toolkit, under review, expects due diligence on accuracy and bias before purchase. National Cyber Security Centre guidance says to build a right to audit into contracts and to exercise it.

**Reading the answer.** The usual gap is AI inside ordinary software, bought without anyone treating it as an AI decision. A high answer needs evidence that a supplier was reassessed after a change.

**A defensible figure.** In the 2024 Bank of England and Financial Conduct Authority survey, third-party implementations made up 65% of AI use cases in human resources and 64% in risk and compliance; across all areas in 2022 the share was 17%.
