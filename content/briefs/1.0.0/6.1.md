# AI threat awareness

**What it measures.** Whether the organisation knows the ways an AI system can be attacked that ordinary software cannot, and has written down which of them apply to each system it runs.

**Why it matters.** A poisoned model or a hijacked assistant still looks as if it is working. The people harmed are those it decides about or acts for: the applicant scored by a manipulated screening tool, the customer whose records an assistant is tricked into disclosing. They cannot see the attack, and often nor can the organisation.

**The UK position.** No UK statute requires an AI threat model. The government's voluntary Code of Practice for the Cyber Security of AI, published in January 2025, says developers and operators shall analyse threats to their systems, and that "Threat modelling should include regular reviews and updates and address AI-specific attacks, such as data poisoning, model inversion, and membership inference." The National Cyber Security Centre warns that language models do not separate instructions from data, so injected instructions may never be fully prevented.

**What good looks like.** A threat model for each system, covering poisoned data, crafted inputs, injected instructions, theft of the model and leakage of what it learned. It includes systems bought from suppliers, is refreshed as new attacks are reported, and is tested by people trying to break the system.

**Reading the answer.** The weak answer is that AI is covered by the existing cyber programme. Ask to see the document, its date, and what last changed because of it.

**A defensible figure.** In a public competition reported by the AI Security Institute, participants submitted 1.8 million prompt-injection attacks against 22 AI agents; over 60,000 succeeded in making an agent break its rules.
