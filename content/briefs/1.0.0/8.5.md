# AI environmental footprint

**What it measures.** Whether the organisation knows how much energy, water and carbon its use of AI accounts for, and whether that shapes the models and suppliers it chooses.

**Why it matters.** The cost of a query is not paid by the person who types it. It falls on communities whose power and drinking water a data centre draws on, and on everyone through emissions. Calling AI use responsible without counting is a claim with nothing behind it.

**The UK position.** No UK law requires a business to report the footprint of the AI it buys. Energy and carbon reporting for large companies covers their own energy use; emissions from purchased services, where cloud and AI sit, are voluntary. Of commercial models, the International Energy Agency says: "This lack of data makes it hard to estimate energy consumption for the field of AI as a whole and for users and companies to make informed choices when it comes to energy efficiency." The EU's AI Act makes providers of general-purpose models document energy consumption, but for regulators on request, not for customers.

**What good looks like.** A short written estimate for the systems that matter, with method and limits stated; suppliers asked for their data; the trend watched; a smaller, task-specific model chosen where it does the job.

**Reading the answer.** Ask how the number was reached. An estimate with a stated method is a sound answer. A footprint dismissed as negligible with no working is not.

**A defensible figure.** Data centres used about 415 terawatt-hours of electricity in 2024, around 1.5% of world consumption, projected to reach about 945 by 2030 (Energy and AI, p.14). That is all data centre use, not AI alone.
