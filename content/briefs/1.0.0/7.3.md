# Oversight by design

**What it measures.** Whether systems are set up so a person can step in: a point to intervene before an output takes effect, reasons on screen, time to act and a way to stop.

**Why it matters.** When a screen shows a conclusion without reasons, and agreeing takes one click while disagreeing takes a form, people agree. The harm falls on people whose cases needed a second look, and on staff held responsible for decisions the design made.

**The UK position.** The UK GDPR requires safeguards to be built into processing when its means are chosen, and anyone subject to a significant, solely automated decision must be able to obtain human intervention. The Information Commission (ICO), in guidance now under review, says controls against automation bias belong in scoping and design, not only in training: "Those in charge of designing the front-end interface of an AI system must understand the needs, thought processes, and behaviours of human reviewers and allow them to effectively intervene." It suggests a confidence score beside each output and testing options with reviewers early. The EU AI Act requires high-risk systems to be designed so that people can oversee, override and interrupt them; on the European Commission's published timetable that duty does not yet apply.

**What good looks like.** A deployment standard: defined intervention points, reasons shown at decision time, overriding as easy as approving, a fall-back to manual working. Interventions are counted, designs tested with reviewers, suppliers held to the standard.

**Reading the answer.** A claim that staff can always override is weak if nobody ever has. Ask to see the reviewer's screen and last quarter's interventions.
