# Oversight capability

**What it measures.** Whether those assigned to oversee AI systems can do the job: trained on the system, given time for each case, and free to overrule it.

**Why it matters.** An overseer with no training, a heavy caseload or a manager who frowns on overrides approves what the system proposes. People affected believe someone checked their case; the overseer carries blame for outcomes they could not change.

**The UK position.** UK data protection law turns on whether human involvement in a significant decision is meaningful, but says little about the person. The Information Commission (ICO), in guidance now under review, says reviewers need authority to override and confidence they will not be penalised, adding: "This authority and confidence cannot be created by policies and training alone: a supportive organisational culture is also crucial". Its audit toolkit expects a manageable caseload and a log of overrides with reasons. Its draft guidance on automated decisions, still in consultation, expects reviewers trained to understand the system's logic, outputs, limitations and risks. The EU AI Act requires deployers of high-risk systems to assign oversight to people with the competence, training, authority and support needed; the European Commission's published text applies this to listed high-risk uses from 2 December 2027.

**What good looks like.** Named overseers with system-specific training, refreshed on change; caseloads that allow attention to each case; written authority to overrule; override rates and sampled re-reviews reported.

**Reading the answer.** A name on a chart is the weak answer. Ask how many cases a reviewer handles daily, and when anyone last overruled the system.

**A defensible figure.** A 2022 peer-reviewed study surveyed 41 policies requiring human oversight of government algorithms and found evidence that people cannot perform the oversight expected.
