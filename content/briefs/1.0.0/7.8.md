# Skill sustainability

**What it measures.** Whether the organisation knows which human skills it must keep as AI takes over tasks, and has a way of keeping them in use.

**Why it matters.** Skills that are not practised fade. Researchers describe the irony: automate the routine and "you deprive the user of the routine opportunities to practice their judgement", so people are unprepared when the exception arrives. Those harmed are the customers and colleagues who rely on a human check that no longer works, and the junior staff who never learn the craft.

**The UK position.** No UK law addresses loss of skill through AI. Employers must take employees' capabilities into account when entrusting tasks, and must train them when new technology brings new risks, but those duties concern health and safety. The Health and Safety Executive expects refresher training for infrequent, complex or safety-critical tasks. Aviation met the problem first: a Civil Aviation Authority paper warned that crews relying on automation might lose manual flying skills, and the United States regulator later asked airlines to make room for manual flying.

**What good looks like.** For each significant automation, a recorded decision on what people must still be able to do and how that is kept up: rotation through manual cases, deliberate practice, checks. Capability is tested, not assumed.

**Reading the answer.** An assurance that people would cope, offered without evidence, is the weak answer. Ask when the manual process was last run.

**A defensible figure.** In a 2026 trial, not yet peer reviewed, 52 software developers learned a new tool; those given AI assistance scored 17% lower on a test of understanding afterwards.
