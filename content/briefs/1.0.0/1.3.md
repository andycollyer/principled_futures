# Policy architecture

**What it measures.** Whether there is a small set of approved, current policies: what AI may be used for, which tools staff may use with what data, and what happens when something goes wrong.

**Why it matters.** Without a rule, each employee decides alone what to put into a tool and how far to trust what comes out. Customers' and colleagues' information goes where nobody agreed it should, and people are affected by outputs nobody checked.

**The UK position.** No UK law requires an AI policy by name. Data protection law requires appropriate data protection policies where proportionate (UK GDPR, Article 24). The UK Corporate Governance Code asks listed company boards to ensure the necessary policies are in place. The Prudential Regulation Authority expects banks' model risk policies to be board-approved and reviewed regularly. The Financial Reporting Council has said: "It is important that there are clear policies and procedures that are embedded across all levels of the organisation." The government's draft self-assessment tool asks whether an AI policy exists, is accessible to all employees and is reviewed. In government-commissioned interviews, very few businesses had one.

**What good looks like.** A board-approved set covering use, escalation and exceptions; a named owner for each; review triggered by changes in law or operations; adherence measured.

**Reading the answer.** Ask a member of staff which tools they may use and what must never go into one. A guess means the policy is unread. The UK's automated decision rules changed on 5 February 2026; an older policy is out of date.

**A defensible figure.** Eleven of 100 listed companies sampled reported having an AI policy or reviewing their code of conduct to cover AI (Financial Reporting Council, November 2025, page 45).
