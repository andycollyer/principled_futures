# Safeguards implementation

**What it measures.** Whether the four safeguards the law attaches to significant, solely automated decisions exist as working processes, each with an owner, a route, a record and a test.

**Why it matters.** A right printed in a privacy notice that nobody is resourced to answer is a broken promise to someone just refused a job, a loan or a service. The mistake stands and nobody is answerable to that person.

**The UK position.** Article 22C of the UK GDPR puts the duty on the controller, which must ensure safeguards are in place: information about the decision, and the means to make representations, obtain human intervention and contest. Article 24 requires it to be able to demonstrate compliance. On recruitment, the Information Commission (ICO) reported: "We also didn’t see widespread evidence that candidates were able to make representations, obtain human intervention or contest decisions." One employer told candidates of the right to human intervention but had no method for handling a request. Its guidance, still a consultation draft, says safeguards must be applied consistently, not at discretion.

**What good looks like.** A named person or team owns each route; a recorded process covers every decision in scope; requests and reviewer overrides are logged with reasons; the process is tested on a sample of cases; results reach senior management.

**Reading the answer.** Ask to see the log. An organisation that cannot show one request and who handled it has a policy, not a safeguard. Monitoring a system's accuracy is not human intervention.

**A defensible figure.** A breach of Article 22C falls in the higher penalty tier: up to £17,500,000 or, for an undertaking, 4% of total worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher (UK GDPR Article 83(5)).
