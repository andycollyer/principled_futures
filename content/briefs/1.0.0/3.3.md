# Bias testing

**What it measures.** Whether the organisation checks what its AI does to different groups: outcomes compared across groups, against thresholds set beforehand, recorded, and repeated after launch.

**Why it matters.** Without testing, the first sign of unfairness is a complaint, by which time people have already been refused a job, a loan or a service because of who they are. Testing also depends on sound group data. Auditing recruitment tools that guessed candidates' gender and ethnicity from their names, the Information Commissioner's Office found: "This inferred information is not accurate enough to monitor bias effectively."

**The UK position.** No UK statute sets a numerical test. The Equality Act 2010 asks whether a practice puts a protected group at a particular disadvantage and whether it is a proportionate means of achieving a legitimate aim. Numbers are evidence towards that question, not the answer. The regulator's guidance, under review since the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025, expects performance to be evaluated group by group and monitored after deployment, as often as the impact warrants. Government assurance guidance calls this a bias audit and expects it repeated.

**What good looks like.** Thresholds chosen and justified in advance. Tests before launch, on a schedule and before each change. Group data collected lawfully, not guessed. Results and remedial action kept and reported to senior management.

**Reading the answer.** Common weak points: a single launch check, and tests covering only sex, ethnicity and age because nothing else is recorded. Small groups may be too few to measure.

**A defensible figure.** Many audited recruitment-tool providers used a four-fifths rule as a minimum: each group's selection rate at least 80% of the highest group's. It comes from United States employment guidelines, not UK law.
